Personal Information
PERSONAL INFORMATION
Name: Henry Norwest (Ducky) Louis
Date of birth: 1884-05-01
Place of birth: Fort Saskatchewan AB Canada
Next of kin: Louis Norwest, Father, Fort Saskatchewan, Saskatchewan
Marital status: single
Occupation (attested): Ranch Hand/Rodeo Performer
Occupation (normalized): Professional Sportsman
Address: Bashaw, Alberta
Religion: Roman Catholic
Date of death: 1918-08-18
Cause of death: Killed in action, shot by a German sniper.
Buried: Warvillers Churchyard Extension, A. 30.
* Henry Louis Norwest was born in Fort Saskatchewan, Alberta, on May 8, 1884. 
* He was of Métis and French-Cree ancestry. 
* Before the First World War, he worked as a ranch hand and rodeo performer.
* Henry Norwest was Cree (Indigenous). 
* Son of Mrs. Genevieve Norwest, of Sacred Heart, Alberta. 
* Henry Louis Norwest was a Métis marksman and one of the most famous Canadian snipers in the First World War. 
* In his nearly three years of service with the 50th Canadian Infantry Battalion, the Lance-corporal achieved a sniping record of 115 fatal shots. 
* He also got the Military Medal and bar, making him one of the 830 members of the CEF to be awarded this double honour.
* Henry Norwest joined the 50th battalion twice, once under the name of Henry Louis and the second time as Henry Norwest. He was discharged the first time for misbehavior (drunkenness). 
* Henry "Ducky" Norwest was killed near Fouquescourt Crucifix Corner. He had targeted another sniper, both fired at the same time, and both were killed.
* Oliver Payne , Ducky's spotter stated: "Suddenly, the instant Ducky fired, a German sniper fired! It missed me but got Ducky right through the head, coming out the other side!




Miltary Information
MILITARY INFORMATION
Regimental number: 435684
Unit/Regiment: Canadian Infantry (Alberta Regiment)
Division: 50th Battalion
Highest Rank: Lance Corporal (50th Battalion)
Rank detail: Lance Corporal, 50th Battalion, Infantry (Army).
Degree of service: Europe
Survived war: no
Battle wounded/killed: Amiens, 1918
Commemoration location: Fort Saskatchewan, AB
Awards: Military Medal, Military Medal First Bar
His experience during the war: Henry Norwest experienced the difficult conditions of trench warfare. He faced constant danger, exhaustion, artillery fire, and the threat of enemy attacks. He used his skills as a ranch hand and rodeo performer from before the war like patience and concentration to help him become an extremely skilled sniper.
* It is recorded that he first enlisted earlier in 1915 under the name Henry Louie, was discharged for drunkenness and misbehavior, and then re-enlisted in Calgary on September 8 as Henry Norwest.
* He was part of the Battle of Vimy Ridge and the Battle of Passchendaele and the Battle of Amiens
* On his cross, it read “It must’ve been a damned good sniper that got Norwest.”
Timeline of his life
TIMELINE OF HIS LIFE
* May 8,1884: He was born in Fort Saskatchewan, Alberta. 
* January 1915: Henry Norwest enlists as Henry Louie. He volunteered for the Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF). He joined the 3rd Canadian Mounted Rifles
* April 1915: His first period of military service lasted only about three months.He was discharged after being disciplined for drunkenness/misconduct.
* May-September 1915: Norwest briefly worked with the Royal North-West Mounted Police.
* September 8, 1915: He enlisted again in Calgary, Alberta. This time he enlisted as Henry Norwest. He joined the 50th Battalion (Calgary), Canadian Expeditionary Force.
* October 27, 1915: Norwest and the 50th Battalion travelled overseas. The battalion sailed from Halifax aboard the SS Orduna.
* November 1915: Henry Norwest and his battalion arrive in England. The 50th Battalion began their training in England before being sent to France. Henry trained with the battalion at Bramshott.
* December 1915: Norwest was disciplined for an alcohol-related incident while training in England and was cancelled several days' pay. He then returned to training. 
* January 1916: During training, he suffered a knee injury/sprain in January 1916 and spent several days in hospital. He recovered and returned to military training.
* Summer 1916: Henry and his battalion complete their military training. 
* August 11, 1916: Henry arrives on the Western Front in France with the 50th Battalion.
* August 19, 1916: The 50th Battalion entered the trenches near Abeele. The battalion began its active combat service.
* September 1916: Henry Norwest was disciplined after being absent without leave for a period during September. He received Field Punishment No. 2.
* October 1916: Henry Norwest takes part in the Battle of the Somme, including the fighting around Flers-Courcelette. This is when his superiors began recognizing his great ability as a marksman. He frequently operated in dangerous areas between the opposing trenches and was also used for reconnaissance
* November 14, 1916: Norwest shot himself on his right foot. He was sent to hospital in Rouen, France. His injury was determined to have been accidental. He was released from hospital later that month. 
* December 3, 1916: Norwest rejoined the 50th Battalion. He returned to active service
* January - April 1917: Henry Norwest completed missions and earned a reputation as an exceptionally skilled sniper. 
* April 9-12, 1917: The Canadian Corps launched its major assault on Vimy Ridge and Henry Norwest fought with them in the Battle of Vimy Ridge. All four Canadian divisions fought together during the battle. Canadian forces captured the German positions. Norwest continued operating as a sniper after the position was captured. He targeted enemy snipers and helped protect Canadian troops from enemy fire. His actions in the battle were later recognized with the Military Medal (MM). 
* Summer–Autumn 1917: Norwest continued serving with the 50th Battalion. He continued working as a sniper and reconnaissance soldier. His confirmed total of successful shots eventually became 115, according to Veterans Affairs Canada.
* Late 1917: The 50th Battalion continued operating in the trenches and taking part in the wider Canadian operations on the Western Front. Norwest survived the major battles of 1917 and continued into the following year.
* January 1918: Norwest and the 50th Battalion continued trench warfare. The battalion spent periods rotating between frontline positions, support areas and training. The winter conditions were difficult, with mud, cold and constant maintenance of trenches. 
* Early 1918: His confirmed total eventually reached 115, the highest recorded total associated with him. He was also valued for reconnaissance and his ability to identify enemy positions. 
* Mid 1918: Norwest received a bar to his Military Medal. A bar was awarded to recognize another act of gallantry after a soldier had already received the Military Medal.
* August 8, 1918: The allied forces launched the Battle of Amiens and Henry Norwest fought with the 50th Battalion Canadian troops who played an important role in the offensive. The Canadian advance moved quickly compared with much of the earlier trench warfare.
* August 12–16, 1918: The 50th Battalion was temporarily moved into a support role. Norwest was sent away from the immediate front for a period of rest. Although he had been temporarily assigned away from the front, Norwest requested to return to the fighting. He believed his skills were needed because enemy snipers were threatening the Canadian positions. He returned to the front and recorded five additional confirmed hits
* August 18, 1918: Norwest and his spotter Oliver Payne took up a position near the Canadian forward lines near Fouquescourt, France. Their task was locating enemy snipers who were threatening Canadian troops. A German sniper located Henry Norwest before he could locate him and Henry was fatally shot. He was 34 years old when he died. His death had a major impact on the 50th Battalion. His commanding officer ordered soldiers to locate the exact place where Norwest had fallen and launch a barage there. 


Battle - Vimy Ridge
BATTLE OF VIMY RIDGE
Where: Vimy, Pas-de-Calais, France
When: April 9, 1917 to April 12, 1917
Why: Vimy Ridge was German-held high ground that formed an important part of the German defensive system. Controlling the ridge gave an important geographical advantage. The Canadians, fighting as part of the larger British effort in what became known as the Battle of Arras, were ordered to seize the high strategic strong point of Vimy Ridge, on the northern flank of the British attack. Attacking the ridge would help divert German resources from the French assault. Capturing this high ground would also give the Allies an important geographic vantage point, with sweeping views over enemy positions to the east. As one Canadian observer noted at the time, “more of the war could be seen (atop Vimy Ridge) than from any other place in France.”
How: The Canadian Corps prepared extensively before the attack. All four Canadian divisions fought together for the first time. The attack began at 5:30 a.m. on Easter Monday, April 9, behind an artillery barrage. Most of the ridge was captured on the first day. Hill 145 was taken on April 10, and the Pimple was captured on April 12. 


A highlight story in the battle: During the Battle of Vimy Ridge, Captain Thain Wendell MacDowell and two of his runners became separated from the rest of their unit. Instead of turning back, the three continued forward and entered a large German dugout. Inside, they unexpectedly faced a large group of German soldiers. MacDowell convinced them to surrender, capturing two officers and 75 soldiers. His actions helped his battalion capture its objective at Hill 145.


Aftermath of the Battle: The First World War and the Battle of Vimy Ridge left their mark on the landscape of France. As well as its people. In 1919, the inhabitants of the village finally returned home. Father Legru described that they found the village “ravaged and completely destroyed by the war.” 


Battle Monument: Vimy Ridge War Memorial
  

Battle Cemetery: Canadian Cemetery No 2
  



Poem: On Vimy Ridge by Ewart Alan Mackintosh (1917
On Vimy Ridge four months ago
We lived and fought, my friends and I,
And watched the kindly dawn come slow,
Peace bringing from the eastern sky.
Now I sit in a quiet town
Remembering how I used to go
Among the dug-outs up and down,
On Vimy Ridge four months ago.


And often sitting here I've seen,
As then I saw them every night,
The friendly faces tired and keen
Across the flickering candle-light,
And heard their laughter gay and clear,
And watched the fires of courage glow
Above the scattered ash of fear,
On Vimy Ridge four months ago.


Oh, friends of mine, where are you now?
Somewhere beneath the troubled sky,
With earth above the quiet brow,
Reader and Stalk for ever lie.
But dead or living out or here
I see the friends I used to know,
And hear the laughter gay and clear,
On Vimy Ridge four months ago.

Reflection Questions
 REFLECTION QUESTIONS
1. What did the soldier investigation reveal about the human experience of war?
Ans: Learning about Henry Norwest taught me that experiencing war is much more horrible and traumatizing than just saying someone was a soldier, or a hero. Before the war, Norwest had a life in Alberta. He worked as a ranch hand and rodeo performer, and he had family and a community. When I learned about these parts of his life, it showed me how the soldiers were not just names or random men who fought in a war. They were ordinary people who left behind their families, jobs, and communities to fight for a horrific war.


Also, I noticed the difference between the achievements and records in a military record and the actual story behind them. The military records tell us his service number, dates, unit, and awards, but they don’t fully explain what his real experience of trench warfare felt like. His Military Medal records tell us that Henry Norwest’s actions were considered brave, but they don’t record how the soldiers had to suffer and live in the trenches that were filled with dirty water. 


His story also showed me that we shouldn’t just remember the achievements of a soldier but also their life and personalities. Henry Norwest is remembered for his military achievements, but he should not be remembered for just those achievements. His story also includes his Métis identity, his life before the war, his relationships with his comrades, and how many of the soldiers who fought with him continued to remember him after his death. Researching him made the cost of war feel more personal because I wasn’t just studying a battle. I was studying the life of a person with a life not too different from ours whose life ended because of the war.




2. Should the memory of this battle be one of celebration or somber remembrance?  Explain. 
Ans: I believe the Battle of Vimy Ridge should be remembered through somber remembrance. Vimy Ridge was an important moment in Canadian military history but there was a huge cost of lives for the battle. Veterans Affairs Canada records 3,598 Canadians killed and 10,602 casualties at Vimy Ridge. Also, the soldiers who did survive were traumatized and were suffering from the effects of living in the trenches for so long.


Henry Norwest was also personally involved in the battle and was awarded the Military Medal for his actions at the Pimple. This was a significant military achievement and became an important part of Canada's history.


Therefore, I think Vimy should be remembered by recognizing both the achievement and the sacrifice. The battle is an important part of Canadian history, but remembering the soldiers as people helps prevent the human cost of war from being forgotten.
